Marine litter: tracking plastic from space to shore

Satellites, ocean models and beach surveys together show where litter comes from, where it travels and where it lands. That knowledge tells clean-up teams where to go and tells policy makers what is working.

Key messages

  1. Most litter on European beaches is plastic, and a short list of single-use items and fishing gear makes up most of it.
  2. Ocean currents and wind decide where floating litter ends up. Copernicus Marine models make those paths predictable.
  3. Satellite imagery can now spot large floating patches, which helps target clean-ups and check whether policies are working.

The problem

Plastic dominates what washes up

Beach litter surveys count every item found along a fixed 100 m stretch of coastline. They are the backbone of EU monitoring under the Marine Strategy Framework Directive (Descriptor 10) and show a clear pattern: plastic, mostly from a few everyday products.

For policyThe Single-Use Plastics Directive (2019/904) targets exactly the items that top the beach counts. Repeated surveys show whether bans and extended producer responsibility reduce them.
For scienceCounts are made with a common protocol so that beaches can be compared across countries. EMODnet Chemistry gathers these surveys into one European database.
For coastal actionA local survey takes about two hours with volunteers. Results feed the same European database the scientists use.
Method detail: how a beach survey works

Surveyors walk a fixed 100 m section from the waterline to the back of the beach, usually four times a year. Every item larger than 2.5 cm is recorded against a joint master list of around 200 categories. Trends are calculated from the median count per survey, which reduces the effect of single storm events.

For policyDrift modelling shows that litter crosses borders. A beach can be polluted by a river in another country, which is why regional sea conventions coordinate monitoring.
For scienceReal drift studies advect virtual particles through modelled surface currents, Stokes drift and windage. Model choices, such as how much wind a floating object feels, change landing sites considerably.
For coastal actionAfter storms with onshore wind, beaches downstream of busy river mouths are the first to fill up. A forecast lets teams plan the clean-up before the litter is buried or washed back out.
Model detail: from currents to particle tracks

Each particle moves by the sum of the surface current, a share of the wind speed (windage, typically 1–3 % for floating plastic) and a random component that stands in for small eddies the model cannot resolve. A particle that reaches the coast is counted as beached. Operational systems run this with hourly current fields from Copernicus Marine and wind from Copernicus atmospheric products.
